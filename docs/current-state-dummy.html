quant-engine — Explained for Absolute Beginners

The same system described in current-state.html, but explained as if you've never heard of quant trading. No jargon without an explanation. Promise.

Written by a quant researcher for a reader with zero trading or programming background. Last updated 2026-07-18. If a sentence here contradicts the technical doc, trust the technical doc.

1 · What is this thing?

In one sentence: quant-engine is a software factory that invents trading strategies, tests them brutally on years of historical data, and — one day, after passing three strict exams — will be allowed to trade real money automatically.

What does it trade? Cryptocurrency contracts — specifically bets on the price of Bitcoin and Ethereum (more on what a "perpetual future" is below). Right now it only trades with pretend money. Real money is deliberately impossible today — the wiring for it literally does not exist yet. That is a safety feature, not a bug.

Who is "trading"? Nobody, in the human sense. The whole point of "quant" (short for quantitative) trading is that a computer follows precise mathematical rules with no human emotion, no gut feelings, and no 3am panic-selling. Humans design the factory; the factory designs and runs the strategies.

The most important fact on this page: the system currently trades zero real dollars. Every trade it has ever made happened either on historical data (a simulation of the past) or in a "paper trading" simulator (a pretend account tracking live prices). There is no code path that can send a real order to a real exchange. That path is planned as the final phase, locked behind an independent go/no-go review.

2 · The words you need (a tiny glossary)

Learn these ten terms and the rest of this page reads easily.

Strategy
A precise rule for when to buy and when to sell. Example of a (toy) strategy: "if the price has risen for 3 days in a row, buy; sell when it drops 2%". Real strategies here are more complex, but they are the same kind of thing: rules, written in math, that a computer can follow without judgement calls.
Perpetual future ("perp")
A type of crypto contract that lets you bet on Bitcoin's price going up or down without ever owning Bitcoin. Think of it as a rolling bet with no expiry date. It's the most heavily traded instrument in crypto. The system trades these on the (simulated) Binance exchange.
Backtest
A time machine for strategies. You take a strategy and ask: "if I had run this rule every day from 2021 to 2024, how much would I have made or lost?" The computer replays history and gives you the answer. A good backtest includes all the annoying real-world costs — fees, funding payments, slippage — because a strategy that only wins when trading is free is worthless.
Overfitting — the #1 enemy
The central disease of this whole field. If you try 10,000 random strategies on the same historical data, a few will look brilliant purely by luck — like flipping 10,000 coins and being amazed that one came up heads 14 times in a row. That "brilliant" strategy will then lose money in real life because its success was coincidence, not insight. An enormous fraction of this codebase exists solely to fight overfitting.
Training / search
The process of having the computer invent and refine thousands of candidate strategies automatically, keeping the promising ones. Like evolution: generate variations, keep the fittest, repeat.
Ensemble
Rather than betting everything on one strategy, the system combines a team of diverse strategies and splits money between them. Same logic as a diversified portfolio: when one is having a bad month, others may not be. The system computes the best team and the best split.
Vintage
The factory's finished, sealed product: a frozen package containing the chosen team of strategies, their money-split weights, and a record of exactly how they were produced. "Sealed" means it's stamped with a tamper-evident fingerprint (a cryptographic hash) — if anyone changes a single byte later, the system notices and refuses to use it. Think: a sealed evidence bag, or a bottled wine vintage — hence the name.
Deterministic
Run the factory twice with the same inputs and the same "random" seed, and you get byte-for-byte identical results. No hidden randomness. Why obsess over this? Because in trading, if you can't reproduce a result, you can't trust it, debug it, or prove where it came from.
Paper trading
Trading with pretend money against real live prices. The full pipeline runs — decisions, orders, risk checks — but the "exchange" is a simulator. It's the dress rehearsal before real money.
Gate
A formal pass/fail exam between phases of the project. The system must prove specific things (e.g. "the strategy still makes money after all costs, on data it has never seen") before the project may advance. There are three: G1, G2, G3 (§7).

3 · What it can do today

The software ships as two programs plus a website. In beginner terms:

ThingWhat it is, plainly
The pipeline tool
qe-cli		 A command-line program (you type commands, it works) with four jobs:
  • train — invent strategies, test them, pick a team, sit the G1 exam, and seal the result into a vintage. This is the whole factory in one command.
  • backtest — take a sealed vintage and replay it over any slice of history: "how would this team have done in 2022?" Produces a detailed report card (returns, worst losing streak, win rate, every single trade).
  • ingest — download and organize historical market data into the local library.
  • evolve — the newest job: a robot that invents brand-new market indicators (measurement formulas) from scratch. Explained in §8.
The control room
qe-server		 A small web server that wraps the pipeline tool so you can drive it from a browser instead of a terminal. It handles who's allowed in (you sign in with Google, and only pre-approved email addresses work), starts jobs, babysits them, and stores the results.
The website
web/		 The browser dashboard itself: log in, click "new backtest", watch a training run's progress live, browse results with charts, and review the formula-inventing robot's output. The screens for live trading exist but are deliberately switched off — they belong to the final phase.
Also built (but only exercised by automated tests so far): the entire "live trading loop" — the machinery that would watch real-time prices, make decisions, check risk limits, and place orders. All of it ends at a simulator instead of a real exchange, and it's guarded by a kill switch (a big red button that halts everything). Roughly 1,100 automated tests check all of this works, all currently passing.

4 · How it works — the story of one strategy's life

Here is the journey from raw data to (eventual) live trading, told as a story. Each numbered step is a real stage in the pipeline.

① Collect the history. Download years of Bitcoin/Ethereum price history — every hour's open, high, low, close, plus the fees and funding payments that perps charge. Garbage in, garbage out, so the data is cross-checked from multiple sources for errors.

② & ③ Clean and shelve it. Reconcile the sources, then file everything into a fast local library (a lightweight embedded database — no big database server needed).

④ Compute the measurements. From raw prices, compute indicators — standardized measurements like "how fast has the price been moving?" or "how wild are the swings lately?". These are the ingredients strategies are built from.

⑤ Evolve strategies. The creative heart. The computer generates thousands of candidate strategies, scores each with careful backtests, then breeds/mutates the best performers over many generations — survival of the fittest, for trading rules. Crucially it uses a technique (called MAP-Elites) that keeps a diverse zoo of good strategies with different personalities, rather than one champion — because one champion is exactly how you end up overfit.

⑥ Assemble the team. From the zoo, pick a complementary team and decide how to split money between them, preferring members whose bad days don't coincide.

⑦ Seal the vintage. Freeze the team + weights + full provenance into a tamper-evident package. From here on, nothing about it can change silently.

Exam G1. Test the sealed vintage on a chunk of history it was never allowed to see during training — after all trading costs, with statistical corrections for "you tried thousands of things, some look good by luck". Fail → back to the drawing board. Pass → eligible for the next phase.

The live loop (built, paper-only). A separate program loads the sealed vintage (read-only), warms itself up on recent history, switches to live price feeds, and runs the strategies in real time — risk checks, position tracking, kill switch and all — with every order going to the pretend exchange. Exam G2 checks the live loop's decisions match the simulator's expectations exactly, with zero real orders. That exam has been passed.

Exam G3 (future). An independent human go/no-go review, then real money — starting tiny, with hard caps, ramping up in stages. Not started.

5 · The robot chef analogy (how the pieces fit)

The codebase is split into 26 separate building blocks ("crates" in Rust jargon — think LEGO bricks of code). You don't need the list; you need the shape. Picture a restaurant company:

Restaurant worldquant-engine world
The test kitchen — where new recipes are invented and taste-tested on historical diners' preferences. The training side: data ingestion, the strategy-evolving search, the team assembler.
The restaurant — where the finalized menu is actually cooked and served, live, under health-and-safety rules. The runtime side: live price feeds, real-time strategy evaluation, risk checks, order handling (currently ending at the pretend exchange).
The recipe binder — the sealed, signed-off menu handed from the test kitchen to the restaurant. The restaurant can read it, never edit it. The vintage — the sealed strategy package. It is the only thing that crosses from training to runtime.
The shared cookbook of basic techniques — knife skills, sauces — used identically by both the test kitchen and the restaurant. The shared spine: the small core of code (basic data types + the indicator/strategy definitions) that both sides use, guaranteeing a strategy behaves identically in backtests and live. If it didn't, your backtest would be a lie.
Head office — schedules everything, hires nobody's imagination. The command-line tool and the web control room, which are the only places allowed to touch both worlds.

6 · Why the "no peeking" rule matters more than anything

Two iron rules are enforced by automated tests that fail the build if anyone breaks them. They sound bureaucratic; they are actually the intellectual core of the project.

Rule ① — the information firewall

The strategy-inventing search is forbidden from seeing live or portfolio results. Analogy: a teacher grading student essays must never know which student is their own child. If the search could peek at how strategies do "for real", it would — subtly, automatically — start optimizing for the peeked answers, and every safeguard against overfitting collapses. So the code that searches literally cannot import the code that trades. A test checks this on every change.

Rule ② — the clean handoff

The live-trading side is forbidden from reaching back into the training machinery, and vice versa. The only bridge is the sealed vintage (plain data) plus the shared cookbook. Analogy: the pharmacy dispenses exactly what's on the sealed prescription; it does not phone the lab and ask for tweaks. This guarantees what was tested is exactly what runs.

Why so paranoid? Because the failure mode of quant trading is not dramatic crashes — it's quiet self-deception: a system that looks great on paper for reasons that evaporate with real money. Every firewall here is a precommitment against fooling ourselves.

7 · The three exams (G1, G2, G3)

ExamQuestion it answersStatus
G1 "Is the strategy team actually good, or just lucky?" Tested on untouched holdout history, after all real-world costs, with statistical penalties for the thousands of candidates tried. Like a final exam with questions the student provably never saw, graded on a curve that punishes lucky guessing. passed
G2 "Does the live machinery behave exactly as the simulation predicts?" The full live loop runs against real-time market data, produces would-be orders, and every one is reconciled against the simulator — with nothing actually submitted. A flight simulator check-ride for the trading system. passed
G3 "Do independent humans sign off on going live with real money?" A go/no-go review by people who didn't build it, followed by a staged capital ramp: tiny amounts first, hard caps enforced in code, increased only gradually. not started

8 · The formula-inventing robot (the newest addition)

Recently the system gained a second, more radical creative tool. Until now, strategies were assembled from a fixed menu of human-designed indicators (§4, step ④). The new evolve job removes that limit: it uses a technique called genetic programming to invent entirely new indicator formulas from scratch — breeding random mathematical expressions, keeping ones that seem to measure something genuinely predictive, discarding the rest, over many generations.

Why this is powerful and dangerous. A machine that invents formulas is an overfitting machine par excellence — with enough random formulas, some will "predict" the past perfectly and mean nothing. So this subsystem is wrapped in more red tape than anything else in the codebase, and that red tape is the feature.

The safeguards, in plain terms:

  • Quarantine. Evolved formulas land in a sealed "formula pool" — a separate artefact the live-trading side never loads. Research output, not a tradeable product.
  • Brutal statistical vetting. Each formula must survive tests designed to detect luck masquerading as skill, plus practicality checks (would it survive trading costs? does it trade too frantically? does it work at realistic size?). At most 16 survivors are frozen into a pool.
  • Human governance with teeth. For a pool to ever be blessed for production use, the rules require: named roles (only designated approvers can approve), two different approvers neither of whom launched the run, and a tamper-evident audit log — every decision chained together cryptographically so history can't be quietly rewritten. The server, not the user, decides whether sealing is allowed, by checking a long list of hard requirements.
  • Currently fail-closed on purpose. As of today, even a perfect pool cannot be sealed for production, because one category of required evidence isn't produced by the pipeline yet. The designers chose to arm the lock before building the key. Research and inspection work end-to-end; production blessing is deliberately impossible until the evidence wiring lands.

9 · How far along is it?

The project runs in phases, each ending at an exam. Score so far: 80 planned work items completed, plus a 24-item hardening program delivered in July 2026.

Phase 0Foundations — project skeleton, configuration, data storage, logging, the reproducibility machinery.complete
Phase 1The strategy factory — data in, strategies evolved, team assembled, vintage sealed, G1 exam passed.complete
Phase 2The live loop — real-time feeds, risk checks, kill switch, pretend exchange, G2 exam passed.complete
Phase "PreP3"The control room — the web dashboard for running and reviewing everything from a browser.complete

Phase 3Real money — order submission to a real exchange, accounting and attribution, monitoring, on-call runbooks, the G3 human sign-off, staged capital ramp. not started

Known loose ends the team has explicitly recorded (none blocks current use): finish the evidence wiring for the formula robot's production path (§8); a handful of robustness chores on the control room (e.g. if the server restarts mid-job, mark the orphaned job as failed rather than "running forever"); some security hardening before exposing the dashboard beyond a trusted network; and switching the repository's automated checks back on (they were temporarily disabled during the July program — a known follow-up).

10 · "So… can it make me money?"

Not today, by design. There is no way to connect this system to real money right now — the final phase (real order submission, monitoring, human go/no-go, capped capital ramp) has not been started. Anyone describing this as a working money-making machine today would be wrong.

What is genuinely impressive, verifiably: the discipline. Every result is reproducible to the byte. The strategy search is physically firewalled from information that would let it cheat. Backtests charge every realistic cost, so no strategy is ranked on profits that wouldn't survive contact with reality. Statistical luck-detectors are built into the exams, not bolted on. And the most dangerous new capability (the formula robot) shipped locked, with the key deliberately not yet cut. In a field where the default failure is self-deception, this codebase is built like it knows that. The engineering is real; the product is mid-build.
